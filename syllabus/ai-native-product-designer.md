# Part I — Introduction to the Profession

## What a product designer does

A product designer shapes how a digital product works, looks and feels — and whether it solves a real problem for real people in a way that also serves the business. The role spans:

1. **Understanding** — users, their problems and contexts; the business and its constraints; technology and its possibilities.
2. **Defining** — which problem to solve, for whom, and what success looks like.
3. **Shaping** — flows, structure, interactions, content, visual design and systems.
4. **Validating** — testing ideas with users and data before and after they ship.
5. **Shipping** — collaborating with engineering and product to deliver at high quality, and iterating after launch.

Product design differs from adjacent roles: it is broader than visual/UI design, more delivery-oriented than pure UX research, and more user-and-craft-focused than product management — though in AI-era teams these boundaries increasingly overlap.

## What "AI-native" adds

Between 2023 and 2026, AI changed product design in two ways:

1. **AI as the material (what you design).** Products now contain language models and agents whose behaviour is probabilistic, open-ended and sometimes wrong. Designers must design for conversation, delegation, uncertainty, trust, explainability, latency, cost, and failure — and decide when AI should *not* be used at all. Interaction paradigms are shifting from "user operates tool" to "user directs, supervises and collaborates with an agent." Reference works such as Google's People + AI Guidebook, Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines for generative AI, and pattern libraries such as Emily Campbell's *Shape of AI* have become core reading.
2. **AI as the collaborator (how you design).** AI accelerates research synthesis, ideation, visual exploration, copy, documentation and — most significantly — prototyping in real code. Tools such as Figma Make and the Figma agent, Figma's code layers (announced at Config 2026), v0, Lovable, Claude and Cursor let designers build working prototypes that behave like the real product. Hiring signals from leading AI companies explicitly ask product designers to prototype with front-end code, and "design engineer" roles are growing.

**What does not change:** taste, judgment, problem framing, empathy, systems thinking and visual craft become *more* valuable, because generating options is now cheap and choosing well is the scarce skill. AI produces average work quickly; exceptional designers know why it is average and how to make it excellent.

## What distinguishes exceptional product designers

| Average | Exceptional |
|---|---|
| Starts in Figma | Starts with the problem, the user and the evidence |
| Makes screens | Designs systems, flows and behaviours — including what happens when things fail |
| Follows trends | Has taste grounded in principles; knows why a design works |
| Treats accessibility as a checklist | Designs inclusively from the start |
| Hands off static files | Prototypes behaviour, often in code; collaborates with engineers continuously |
| Talks about pixels | Connects design decisions to user outcomes and business metrics |
| Uses AI to make more screens | Uses AI to explore wider, test faster and build higher-fidelity prototypes — then applies judgment |
| Designs AI as a chat box | Designs AI as a capability with appropriate autonomy, transparency, feedback and control |

## Career levels and what each expects

- **Junior / Associate:** Executes well-defined design tasks with guidance; solid visual and interaction fundamentals; Figma fluency; follows the design system; participates in research.
- **Mid-level:** Owns features end to end; runs research and usability tests; designs complete flows with all states; collaborates closely with engineers; contributes to the design system.
- **Senior:** Owns problem spaces; frames problems, influences roadmap; designs complex systems; prototypes in high fidelity (increasingly in code); mentors; designs AI features with appropriate trust and failure handling.
- **Staff / Principal:** Shapes product strategy and design direction across teams; defines interaction paradigms (e.g., how agents work across a product); sets quality standards; drives design systems at scale; represents design at the leadership level.
- **Design manager / Director / Head of Design:** Builds and develops teams, design operations, culture and the design organization's strategy.

## Timeless vs. tooling

Each domain separates **timeless foundations** (perception, psychology, principles, process, ethics) from **current tools (2026 snapshot)** (specific apps and AI products), so you invest in what lasts and stay current on what changes.

## A rule for using AI while learning

- **Stages 1–2:** Use AI as a tutor and critic, not as a generator. Draw, lay out and type-set by hand (or by direct manipulation in Figma). Visual judgment is trained by making many deliberate decisions yourself.
- **Stages 3–5:** Use AI to accelerate research synthesis and explore alternatives — always comparing against your own first attempt and verifying against real user evidence.
- **Stage 6 onward:** Learn to direct AI deliberately as a design collaborator and prototyping engine.
- Never present AI-synthesized research or AI-generated personas as real user evidence.

# Part III — The Complete Learning Sequence

## The dependency chain

```
STAGE 1  SEEING AND MAKING
  Design foundations & visual literacy ──► Figma fundamentals ──► Visual design
  (principles, Gestalt, hierarchy)          (frames, shapes, text,   (typography, color, grids,
                                             Auto Layout basics)      layout, icons, imagery)
                         │
STAGE 2  UNDERSTANDING HUMANS                ▼
  Human psychology & HCI ──► Accessibility & inclusive design
                         │
STAGE 3  UX DESIGN                           ▼
  UX process ──► User research ──► Synthesis, IA & flows ──► Interaction design,
  wireframing & prototyping ──► Content design ──► Usability testing & evaluation
                         │
STAGE 4  UI AND SYSTEMS                      ▼
  UI design (patterns, forms, tables, dashboards, data viz, responsive)
  ──► Motion & micro-interactions ──► Advanced Figma ──► Design systems
                         │
STAGE 5  BUILDING AND BUSINESS               ▼
  Design engineering (HTML/CSS/JS, components, design-to-code)
  ──► Product thinking, strategy & business ──► Metrics, analytics & experimentation
                         │
STAGE 6  AI                                  ▼
  AI foundations ──► AI-native product design ──► AI-assisted design workflows
                         │
STAGE 7  MASTERY                             ▼
  Practice, communication & collaboration ──► Leadership & ethics
```

## Why this order

1. **Seeing before making.** Visual literacy (principles, Gestalt, hierarchy) is the lens through which every later design decision is judged.
2. **Basic Figma early, advanced Figma later.** You need a tool to practise visual design, but advanced component and variable systems only make sense after you understand UI patterns and systems thinking.
3. **Humans before UX process.** Perception, cognition and accessibility explain *why* usability principles work; without them, UX methods become rituals.
4. **Research before solutions.** Problem understanding precedes structure (IA, flows), which precedes interaction, which precedes visual UI.
5. **UI patterns before design systems.** You systematize what you already know how to design well.
6. **Design engineering after systems.** Code becomes meaningful once you think in components, states and tokens.
7. **Business and metrics after craft.** Strategic influence requires credible craft first; strategy without craft produces slideware.
8. **AI after the full foundation.** Designing AI products well depends on research, interaction design, content design, systems and metrics. AI-assisted workflows are introduced lightly early, but mastered when you can judge the output.

## Building while learning

Every stage should end with a piece of work you could show and explain (not a schedule):
- Stage 1: a set of poster/layout studies and a type-and-color system for a fictional brand.
- Stage 3: an end-to-end case study — research, synthesis, flows, prototype, usability test findings, iteration.
- Stage 4: a complex product interface (dashboard or data-heavy tool) built on a small, documented design system with tokens and components.
- Stage 5: a coded, responsive prototype of a key flow, plus a product brief with metrics and an experiment plan.
- Stage 6: an AI-native feature — e.g., an agent that completes a multi-step task with approvals, uncertainty states, citations, feedback and failure handling — designed, prototyped in code with a real model, and tested with users.

## Domain 1: Design Foundations and Visual Literacy

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D3 · **Prerequisites:** None. Curiosity, a sketchbook and a computer.

**Why it matters:** Every interface is a visual composition. Visual literacy — the ability to see, name and reason about what makes a composition work — is the foundation for all later craft and critique.

### 1.1 Introduction to Design

#### 1.1.1 What design is
- **Definitions and scope**
  - Design as intentional problem-solving under constraints
  - Form and function; aesthetics and usability
  - Design disciplines: graphic, communication, industrial, interaction, UX, UI, product, service, brand, motion, content
- **Design history (essentials)**
  - Bauhaus and modernism: form follows function
  - Swiss/International Typographic Style: grids, clarity, objectivity
  - Dieter Rams' ten principles of good design
  - Human factors and ergonomics origins
  - The personal computer and GUI (Xerox PARC, Apple Macintosh)
  - The web, responsive design and mobile
  - Flat design, material design and design systems
  - The AI era: generative tools and agentic products
- **Design thinking**
  - Double Diamond (discover, define, develop, deliver)
  - Divergent vs. convergent thinking
  - Iteration and feedback loops

#### 1.1.2 How designers see
- Observing and analysing existing designs
- Building a reference library (screens, layouts, typography, interactions)
- Learning by recreation ("copywork") vs. imitation
- Developing taste: exposure, analysis, practice, critique

### 1.2 Design Principles

#### 1.2.1 Core principles of composition
- **Balance**
  - Symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial balance
  - Visual weight: size, color, density, position
- **Contrast**
  - Size, color, value (light/dark), weight, shape, texture, direction
  - Contrast as the engine of hierarchy and attention
- **Alignment**
  - Edge alignment, centre alignment, optical alignment
  - Invisible lines and structure
- **Proximity**
  - Grouping related elements; separating unrelated ones
  - Spacing as meaning
- **Repetition and consistency**
  - Patterns, rhythm, unity
- **Emphasis and focal point**
  - One primary focal point per composition
- **Movement and flow**
  - Directing the eye: reading patterns (Z-pattern, F-pattern), leading lines
- **White space (negative space)**
  - Active vs. passive white space; breathing room; luxury and clarity
- **Scale and proportion**
  - Relative size; ratios (golden ratio, modular scales) as tools, not laws
- **Unity and variety**

#### 1.2.2 Gestalt principles of perception
- Figure–ground
- Proximity
- Similarity
- Continuity
- Closure
- Common region
- Common fate
- Prägnanz (simplicity)
- Symmetry and order
- Uniform connectedness
- Applying Gestalt to interfaces: grouping cards, form fields, navigation, lists

#### 1.2.3 Visual hierarchy
- What hierarchy is: ordering information by importance
- Tools of hierarchy: size, weight, color, contrast, position, spacing, depth
- Levels of hierarchy (primary, secondary, tertiary)
- Scanning behaviour and progressive disclosure
- Squint test and blur test
- Hierarchy in interfaces: headings, actions, data emphasis

### 1.3 Visual Communication

#### 1.3.1 Communicating with visuals
- Semiotics basics: signs, symbols, icons, indices
- Visual metaphors and their cultural specificity
- Denotation and connotation
- Tone and mood: how shape, color and type express personality
- Clarity vs. expression trade-offs

#### 1.3.2 Drawing and sketching
- Sketching as thinking (not art)
- Basic shapes, lines and perspective for UI sketches
- Thumbnail sketches and rapid ideation
- Storyboarding

### 1.4 Visual Literacy Practice

#### 1.4.1 Critique vocabulary
- Describing what you see before judging it
- Naming principles at work (or violated)
- Separating personal preference from effectiveness

## Domain 2: Design Tools I — Figma Fundamentals

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D3 for fundamentals · **Prerequisites:** Basic computer literacy. Learn alongside Domains 1 and 3.

**Why it matters:** Figma is the industry-standard collaborative interface design tool. Basic fluency lets you practise visual design efficiently; advanced Figma (Domain 14) comes after you understand UI and systems.

### 2.1 Figma Basics

#### 2.1.1 The workspace
- Files, pages, canvas, layers panel, properties panel
- Navigation: zoom, pan, selection, keyboard shortcuts
- Figma Design vs. FigJam (whiteboarding) vs. Figma Slides vs. Figma Make vs. Figma Sites vs. Figma Draw vs. Figma Buzz — what each is for
- Teams, projects, file organization, version history

#### 2.1.2 Frames and layers
- Frames vs. groups vs. sections
- Device presets and custom frame sizes
- Nested frames; clipping content
- Layer naming and organization (a professional habit from day one)

#### 2.1.3 Shapes, vectors and images
- Rectangles, ellipses, polygons, lines
- Pen tool and vector networks
- Boolean operations (union, subtract, intersect, exclude)
- Fills, strokes, effects (shadows, blurs)
- Corner radius and corner smoothing
- Images: placing, cropping, fill modes
- Masks

#### 2.1.4 Text
- Text layers, auto width/height, fixed size
- Font, size, weight, line height, letter spacing, paragraph spacing
- Text styles (introduction)
- OpenType features

#### 2.1.5 Constraints and introductory Auto Layout
- Constraints (pinning to edges, scaling)
- Auto Layout basics: direction, spacing, padding, alignment
- Hug contents vs. fill container vs. fixed sizing
- Why Auto Layout mirrors CSS flexbox

#### 2.1.6 Styles and basic components
- Color styles and text styles
- Creating simple components and instances
- Overrides

#### 2.1.7 Basic prototyping and sharing
- Linking frames; interactions (on click, on hover)
- Simple transitions
- Presenting prototypes; sharing links; comments

### Timeless vs. tools — Domain 2

- **Timeless:** layered composition, constraint-based layout, reusable components, naming discipline.
- **Current (2026):** Figma (with Make, Sites, Draw, Buzz, Slides, the Figma agent and code layers); alternatives include Penpot (open source), Sketch (macOS), Framer (design-to-site), and code-first tools.

## Domain 3: Visual Design

**Label:** [Essential] (3.1–3.7), [Core-Senior] (3.8–3.9) · **Depth target:** D3 (D4 for senior visual/brand-heavy roles) · **Prerequisites:** Domains 1–2.

**Why it matters:** Visual design is how products communicate quality, hierarchy and brand. AI tools can generate passable visuals instantly; distinctive, coherent visual systems still require trained designers.

### 3.1 Typography

#### 3.1.1 Type anatomy and classification
- **Anatomy**
  - Baseline, x-height, cap height, ascenders, descenders
  - Stroke, stem, serif, terminal, counter, bowl, aperture
  - Kerning vs. tracking vs. leading
- **Classification**
  - Serif (old style, transitional, didone, slab), sans-serif (grotesque, neo-grotesque, humanist, geometric), monospace, display, script
  - Typeface vs. font vs. font family
  - Variable fonts and their axes (weight, width, optical size)

#### 3.1.2 Typography for reading
- Readability vs. legibility
- Font size and the reading distance of devices
- Line length (measure): roughly 45–75 characters
- Line height (leading) relative to size and measure
- Paragraph spacing; alignment (avoid justified text on screens)
- Contrast and weight for readability
- Typesetting details: quotes, apostrophes, dashes, ellipses, non-breaking spaces, numerals (tabular vs. proportional, lining vs. old-style)

#### 3.1.3 Typographic systems
- Type scales: modular scales and ratios
- Establishing hierarchy with as few sizes/weights as possible
- Pairing typefaces (contrast and harmony; one family is often enough)
- Responsive and fluid typography
- Interface typography: system fonts (SF Pro, Roboto, Segoe), UI-optimized faces (Inter, etc.)
- Internationalization: scripts, language support, text expansion, RTL
- Licensing fonts

### 3.2 Color

#### 3.2.1 Color theory
- **Perception**
  - How the eye perceives color; light vs. pigment
  - Additive (RGB) vs. subtractive (CMYK) color
- **Color properties**
  - Hue, saturation (chroma), lightness (value)
  - Color models: RGB, HSL, HSB; perceptual models (OKLCH, LAB) and why they produce more uniform palettes
  - Color temperature
- **Harmony**
  - Color wheel relationships: monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic
  - Simultaneous contrast and color interaction (Albers)
- **Color psychology and culture**
  - Associations and their cultural variability; avoiding simplistic rules

#### 3.2.2 Color in interfaces
- Building UI palettes: neutrals (most of the interface), primary/brand, semantic colors (success, warning, error, info)
- Shades and tints: generating scales (e.g., 50–950)
- Using color for meaning sparingly; color for hierarchy
- Contrast ratios and WCAG requirements (4.5:1 text, 3:1 large text/UI components); APCA as an emerging model
- Never relying on color alone to convey meaning
- Color blindness simulation
- Dark mode: not inverted light mode; elevation through lightness; desaturated colors
- Wide-gamut color (Display P3) awareness
- Color tokens (primitive → semantic) — introduced here, formalized in Domain 15

### 3.3 Layout and Grids

#### 3.3.1 Grids
- History: Swiss grid systems
- Column grids, gutters, margins
- Modular and baseline grids
- 12-column grids and responsive breakpoints
- Hierarchical grids for interfaces
- Breaking the grid deliberately

#### 3.3.2 Layout
- Layout patterns: single column, sidebar, split screen, cards, masonry, dashboard
- Content-first layout
- Above-the-fold myths and scrolling behaviour
- Density: compact vs. comfortable
- Responsive layout thinking (deepened in Domain 12)

### 3.4 Spacing and Proportion

#### 3.4.1 Spacing systems
- 4-point and 8-point spacing systems
- Spacing scales (tokens)
- Internal vs. external spacing (padding vs. margin)
- Proximity-driven spacing (the more related, the closer)
- Optical vs. mathematical spacing
- Vertical rhythm

#### 3.4.2 Proportion
- Ratios; aspect ratios for media
- Sizing relationships between elements (icons to text, buttons to inputs)

### 3.5 Shape, Depth and Surface

#### 3.5.1 Form
- Basic shapes and their associations
- Corner radius systems and nested radius rules
- Borders vs. shadows vs. background contrast for separation
- Elevation and depth: shadow systems, layering, z-order
- Skeuomorphism, flat design, neumorphism, glassmorphism and translucent materials (e.g., Apple's Liquid Glass) — understanding trends vs. principles
- Texture and noise

### 3.6 Iconography

#### 3.6.1 Icons
- Icon types: system/UI icons, product icons, illustrative icons
- Icon grids, keylines, optical sizing
- Stroke weight, corner treatments, consistency
- Filled vs. outlined states
- Icon + label vs. icon alone (when icons need labels)
- Recognizability and cultural meaning
- Icon libraries (Material Symbols, SF Symbols, Lucide, Phosphor) and when to customize
- Accessible icons (text alternatives)

### 3.7 Imagery and Illustration

#### 3.7.1 Imagery
- Photography direction: subject, composition, lighting, authenticity, diversity
- Image cropping and focal points
- Image treatment consistency
- Stock vs. custom vs. AI-generated imagery — quality, ethics, rights and disclosure

#### 3.7.2 Illustration
- Illustration styles and systems
- Spot illustrations, empty states, onboarding
- Character design consistency
- Vector illustration in Figma; generative illustration with style control

### 3.8 Art Direction and Brand (Core-Senior)

#### 3.8.1 Art direction
- Defining a visual direction: moodboards, references, principles
- Brand identity basics: logo, color, type, voice, imagery
- Brand expression in product UI (restraint vs. expressiveness)
- Directing illustrators, photographers and AI image tools to a consistent style
- Visual language documentation

### 3.9 Visual Storytelling and Advanced Visual Design (Core-Senior)

#### 3.9.1 Storytelling
- Narrative structure in landing pages, onboarding and presentations
- Sequencing and pacing
- Data storytelling (deepened in Domain 12)

#### 3.9.2 Advanced craft
- Detail and polish: optical alignment, pixel-snapping, consistent radii, rendering
- Designing for multiple densities and platforms
- Expressive interfaces: when to break conventions
- Developing a personal and team visual taste — studying exemplary products (e.g., Linear, Stripe, Apple, Airbnb, Vercel, Arc, Things, Raycast)

## Domain 4: Human Psychology and Human-Computer Interaction

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D3 · **Prerequisites:** Domain 1.

**Why it matters:** Usability principles are not opinions — they follow from how human perception, attention, memory and decision-making work. Understanding them lets you predict problems before testing, explain decisions to stakeholders, and design AI interactions that respect human limits.

### 4.1 Human-Computer Interaction Foundations

#### 4.1.1 What HCI is
- HCI as an interdisciplinary field (computer science, psychology, design, sociology)
- Key history: Engelbart's demo, Xerox PARC, direct manipulation (Shneiderman), the GUI, the web, mobile/touch, voice, AI
- Models of interaction: Norman's seven stages of action; gulfs of execution and evaluation
- Affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings, feedback, conceptual models (Norman)
- Direct manipulation vs. command-based vs. conversational vs. delegated (agentic) interaction

#### 4.1.2 Classic HCI laws and models
- Fitts's law (target size and distance)
- Hick's law (choice count and decision time)
- Steering law (navigating tunnels like nested menus)
- GOMS and keystroke-level model (awareness)
- Power law of practice
- Response time limits: 0.1s (instant), 1s (flow of thought), 10s (attention limit) — directly relevant to AI latency design

### 4.2 Human Perception

#### 4.2.1 Visual perception
- The eye: foveal vs. peripheral vision
- Pre-attentive processing (color, size, motion, orientation)
- Gestalt principles (from Domain 1) as perception science
- Change blindness and inattentional blindness
- Banner blindness
- Visual search and scanning patterns (F-pattern, layer-cake, spotted patterns)
- Motion perception and attention capture

#### 4.2.2 Other senses in interfaces
- Auditory feedback and earcons
- Haptics
- Multimodal perception

### 4.3 Cognitive Psychology

#### 4.3.1 Attention and memory
- Selective and divided attention; limited attentional resources
- Working memory limits (Miller's "7±2" and the more modern ~4 chunks)
- Chunking
- Long-term memory; recognition vs. recall (recognition is easier — menus beat commands for novices)
- Interruptions and task resumption

#### 4.3.2 Cognitive load
- Intrinsic, extraneous and germane load
- Reducing extraneous load: clarity, consistency, defaults, progressive disclosure
- Cognitive load in AI interfaces: reading long outputs, verifying claims, writing prompts (the "articulation barrier")

#### 4.3.3 Mental models
- How users form models of systems
- Mismatches between designer's model, system image and user's model
- Jakob's law: users expect your product to work like the others they use
- Mental models of AI: anthropomorphism, over-trust, under-trust, misconceptions about memory and capabilities

#### 4.3.4 Learning and expertise
- Novice-to-expert progression; designing for both (shortcuts, progressive disclosure)
- Habit formation
- Learnability vs. efficiency

### 4.4 Behavioural Psychology and Decision-Making

#### 4.4.1 How people decide
- Dual-process theory: fast/intuitive (System 1) vs. slow/deliberate (System 2)
- Bounded rationality and satisficing
- Heuristics and cognitive biases relevant to design:
  - Anchoring, framing, default effect, loss aversion
  - Choice overload (paradox of choice)
  - Social proof, authority, scarcity
  - Peak-end rule
  - Zeigarnik effect, goal-gradient effect
  - Serial position effect
  - Automation bias (over-reliance on automated suggestions — critical for AI)
- Motivation: intrinsic vs. extrinsic; self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness)
- Behaviour models: Fogg Behavior Model (motivation, ability, prompt); habit loops (awareness)

#### 4.4.2 Ethics of persuasion
- Nudges vs. dark patterns (deceptive design)
- Types of deceptive patterns: confirmshaming, roach motel, sneak into basket, forced continuity, disguised ads, privacy zuckering
- Regulation: EU Digital Services Act, consumer protection laws, FTC enforcement
- Designing for user autonomy and long-term trust

### 4.5 Emotion and Experience

#### 4.5.1 Emotional design
- Visceral, behavioural, reflective levels (Norman)
- Delight vs. usability; the aesthetic-usability effect
- Trust formation; credibility cues
- Frustration and error recovery
- Peak moments and memorable experiences

## Domain 5: Accessibility and Inclusive Design

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D3 (D4 for design-system and senior designers) · **Prerequisites:** Domains 3, 4.

**Why it matters:** Over a billion people live with disabilities, and everyone experiences situational limitations. Accessibility is a legal requirement in many markets (including the European Accessibility Act, in force since June 2025, and the ADA in the US) and a quality marker. Most accessibility defects originate in design, not code.

### 5.1 Accessibility Foundations

#### 5.1.1 Understanding disability
- Medical vs. social models of disability
- Permanent, temporary and situational impairments (Microsoft's Persona Spectrum)
- Disability categories: visual, auditory, motor, cognitive/learning, speech, neurological, vestibular
- Assistive technologies and how people use them: screen readers, magnifiers, switch access, voice control, captions, keyboard-only navigation

#### 5.1.2 Standards and law
- WCAG 2.2: POUR principles (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust), levels A/AA/AAA
- WCAG 3 (in development — awareness)
- Legal frameworks: ADA, Section 508, European Accessibility Act, EN 301 549, and national equivalents
- Accessibility conformance reports (VPAT/ACR)

### 5.2 Designing Accessibly

#### 5.2.1 Visual accessibility
- Color contrast for text, icons and UI components
- Not relying on color alone (icons, patterns, text labels)
- Text size, resizing, reflow and zoom
- Focus indicators: visible, high-contrast, consistent (WCAG 2.2 focus requirements)
- Dark mode and high-contrast/forced-colors considerations

#### 5.2.2 Interaction accessibility
- Target sizes (WCAG 2.2 minimum target size; platform guidance of 44×44pt / 48×48dp)
- Keyboard navigation design: focus order, skip links, shortcuts
- Alternatives to complex gestures (drag, multi-finger) — WCAG 2.2 dragging movements
- Timeouts and time limits
- Motion: reduced-motion alternatives; avoiding vestibular triggers and flashing content

#### 5.2.3 Content and structure accessibility
- Headings and landmarks as navigation for screen-reader users
- Annotating designs for accessibility: reading order, heading levels, landmarks, alt text, accessible names, focus order, states
- Writing alt text (informative, decorative, functional, complex images)
- Accessible forms: visible labels, instructions, error messages, error prevention
- Accessible data visualization: text alternatives, patterns, data tables
- Captions, transcripts and audio descriptions
- Plain language and cognitive accessibility: consistent navigation, predictable behaviour, help availability

### 5.3 Inclusive Design

#### 5.3.1 Principles of inclusive design
- "Solve for one, extend to many" (Microsoft Inclusive Design)
- Recognizing exclusion
- Learning from diversity; co-designing with excluded communities
- Inclusive language and representation in content and imagery
- Designing for age, literacy, language, culture, bandwidth and device constraints
- Gender-inclusive forms and data collection
- Global and localization-ready design (text expansion, RTL, cultural conventions)

### 5.4 Testing Accessibility as a Designer

#### 5.4.1 Methods
- Contrast and color-blindness checks in Figma
- Keyboard walkthroughs of prototypes
- Basic screen reader use (VoiceOver, NVDA) to understand the experience
- Accessibility annotation reviews with engineers
- Usability testing with disabled participants

### 5.5 Accessibility of AI Experiences

#### 5.5.1 AI and accessibility
- AI as an accessibility enabler (descriptions, captions, summaries, voice interfaces) — and its risks (inaccuracy)
- Accessible chat and agent interfaces: announcements of streaming content, keyboard access to actions, clear status
- Plain-language AI responses; controlling verbosity
- Avoiding exclusion through voice-only or prompt-only interfaces

## Domain 7: User Research

**Label:** [Essential] (7.1–7.5), [Core-Senior] (7.6) · **Depth target:** D3 · **Prerequisites:** Domains 4, 6.

**Why it matters:** Research is how designers replace assumptions with evidence. AI can accelerate research operations and synthesis, but it cannot replace contact with real people — and synthetic users are not users.

### 7.1 Research Foundations

#### 7.1.1 Research landscape
- Generative (discovery) vs. evaluative research
- Qualitative vs. quantitative research
- Attitudinal (what people say) vs. behavioural (what people do)
- Choosing methods: NN/g's landscape of research methods
- Research questions vs. interview questions
- Research planning: goals, questions, methods, participants, timeline, outputs
- Research ethics: informed consent, privacy, data handling, compensation, vulnerable participants
- Bias in research: confirmation bias, leading questions, sampling bias, observer effect

### 7.2 Qualitative Methods

#### 7.2.1 User interviews
- Recruiting and screening participants
- Writing a discussion guide
- Open-ended, non-leading questions; asking about past behaviour, not hypotheticals
- Follow-ups and probing ("tell me more about…", "the last time…")
- Active listening; comfort with silence
- Note-taking and recording
- Remote vs. in-person
- The Mom Test principles: talk about their life, not your idea

#### 7.2.2 Contextual inquiry and ethnographic methods
- Observing people in their environment
- Master–apprentice model
- Field studies; shadowing
- Diary studies

#### 7.2.3 Other qualitative methods
- Focus groups (and their limitations)
- Co-design and participatory design workshops
- Concept testing
- Card sorting (bridge to IA, Domain 8)

### 7.3 Quantitative Methods

#### 7.3.1 Surveys
- When surveys are appropriate (and when they are not)
- Question types: closed, open, Likert scales, ranking
- Writing unbiased questions; avoiding double-barrelled questions
- Sampling and response bias; sample size basics
- Standardized instruments: SUS (System Usability Scale), UMUX-Lite, NPS (and its criticisms), CSAT, CES

#### 7.3.2 Behavioural data
- Product analytics as research (bridge to Domain 18)
- Funnel analysis, session recordings, heatmaps
- Combining quant (what) with qual (why)

### 7.4 Modelling Users

#### 7.4.1 Personas
- Proto-personas vs. research-based personas
- Behavioural attributes over demographics
- Common persona failures (fictional, stereotyped, unused)
- AI-generated personas: useful for brainstorming, never a substitute for evidence

#### 7.4.2 Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)
- Jobs theory: people "hire" products to make progress in circumstances
- Functional, emotional and social dimensions of jobs
- Job stories ("When…, I want to…, so I can…")
- Switch interviews and the forces of progress (push, pull, anxiety, habit)
- Outcome-driven innovation (awareness)

#### 7.4.3 Other models
- Empathy maps
- User archetypes and behavioural segments
- Mental model diagrams (Indi Young)

### 7.5 Journeys

#### 7.5.1 User journey mapping
- Current-state vs. future-state journey maps
- Stages, actions, thoughts, emotions, pain points, opportunities
- Touchpoints and channels
- Experience maps vs. journey maps vs. service blueprints (bridge to service design)

### 7.6 Research Operations and Scale (Core-Senior)

#### 7.6.1 ResearchOps
- Participant panels and recruitment pipelines
- Research repositories and atomic research (insights tied to evidence)
- Democratizing research: enabling PMs and engineers responsibly
- Continuous discovery habits: weekly customer touchpoints
- AI in research operations: transcription, tagging, synthesis drafts, interview assistance — with verification and privacy safeguards

## Domain 11: Usability Testing and UX Evaluation

### 11.1 Usability Testing

#### 11.1.1 Planning tests
- Test goals and research questions
- Tasks: realistic scenarios, not instructions

- Observing behaviour over opinions
- Recording and note-taking; involving the team as observers

#### 11.1.3 Analysing results
- Severity ratings
- Task success, time on task, errors

- Accessibility audits

### 11.3 Quantitative UX Evaluation

#### 11.3.1 Measuring UX
- Benchmarking usability over time
- UX metrics: task success rate, time on task, error rate, SUS, SEQ

### 11.4 Evaluating AI Experiences

- Testing with live models: variability between sessions; testing multiple runs

## Domain 12: UI Design

**Label:** [Essential] (12.1–12.7), [Core-Senior] (12.8) · **Depth target:** D3 (D4 senior) · **Prerequisites:** Domains 3, 5, 9.

**Why it matters:** UI design turns structure and interaction into a precise, consistent, usable interface. Complex UI — forms, tables, dashboards, data — is where product designers prove their craft and where AI-generated UI most often falls short.

### 12.1 Interface Fundamentals

#### 12.1.1 Anatomy of interfaces
- Screens, views, regions, panels, layers
- Components: atoms of UI (buttons, inputs, checkboxes, toggles, chips, badges, avatars, tooltips)
- Containers: cards, lists, modals, drawers, sheets, popovers
- Visual hierarchy within screens: primary action, secondary actions, content priority
- Consistency with platform conventions (Apple HIG, Material Design, Fluent, web norms)

#### 12.1.2 Buttons and actions
- Button hierarchy: primary, secondary, tertiary, ghost, destructive
- One primary action per view
- Button labels; icon buttons; split buttons
- Button placement conventions per platform
- Disabled vs. hidden vs. enabled-with-explanation

### 12.2 Interface Patterns

#### 12.2.1 Common patterns
- Navigation patterns (from Domain 8)
- Search, filtering, sorting
- Lists and cards
- Modals, dialogs, drawers, popovers — when to use each
- Tabs, accordions, segmented controls
- Pagination vs. infinite scroll vs. "load more"
- Notifications: toasts, banners, inline alerts, badges
- Onboarding patterns: empty states, coach marks, progressive onboarding, templates
- Settings and preferences
- Authentication flows: sign-up, sign-in, passkeys, password reset, MFA
- Checkout and payment flows
- Sharing and permissions
- Undo, history and versioning
- Collaboration patterns: presence, comments, mentions

### 12.3 Forms

#### 12.3.1 Form design
- Single-column layouts; logical grouping
- Labels above fields; never placeholders as labels
- Input types matched to data (and mobile keyboards)
- Required vs. optional indication
- Smart defaults and autofill
- Inline validation timing (on blur, not on every keystroke)
- Error messages adjacent to fields; error summaries for long forms
- Multi-step forms and progress indicators
- Reducing fields: question every field
- Accessibility of forms (Domain 5)
- AI in forms: extracting data from uploaded documents, suggesting values — with visible review

### 12.4 Tables and Data-Dense Interfaces

#### 12.4.1 Table design
- When to use tables vs. lists vs. cards
- Column alignment (numbers right-aligned, text left-aligned)
- Row density options; zebra striping vs. dividers
- Sorting, filtering, column customization, resizing
- Row selection and bulk actions
- Inline editing
- Sticky headers and first columns
- Pagination and virtualization implications
- Responsive tables: priority columns, stacked cards, horizontal scroll
- Empty, loading and error states for tables

### 12.5 Dashboards and Data Visualization

#### 12.5.1 Dashboards
- Dashboard types: operational, analytical, strategic
- Defining the questions a dashboard must answer
- Layout: most important metrics first; progressive drill-down
- KPI cards, trends, comparisons, targets
- Filters and time ranges
- Avoiding "data puke"

#### 12.5.2 Data visualization
- Visual encoding: position, length, angle, area, color, shape (ranked by perceptual accuracy)
- Choosing chart types: bar, line, scatter, area, pie (rarely), heatmap, histogram, box plot, sparkline
- Axes, scales, baselines (start bars at zero), labels, legends (label directly where possible)
- Color in charts: categorical, sequential, diverging palettes; accessibility
- Data-ink ratio and chartjunk (Tufte)
- Annotation and storytelling with data
- Interactive visualizations: tooltips, brushing, zoom
- Accessible charts: text summaries, data tables, patterns
- Visualizing uncertainty (confidence intervals, ranges) — relevant to AI outputs

### 12.6 Responsive and Multi-Platform Interfaces

#### 12.6.1 Responsive design
- Mobile-first thinking
- Breakpoints vs. fluid design; content-driven breakpoints
- Layout adaptation: reflow, reveal, resize, reposition
- Touch vs. pointer considerations
- Responsive typography and spacing

#### 12.6.2 Platform-specific design
- iOS (Apple HIG): navigation stacks, tab bars, sheets, SF Symbols, Dynamic Type, materials
- Android (Material Design 3): navigation bar/rail/drawer, FABs, dynamic color, Material You
- Desktop applications: windows, menus, keyboard shortcuts, density
- Web applications: browser conventions, URLs as state, back button behaviour
- Cross-platform consistency vs. platform fidelity

### 12.7 Theming and Modes

#### 12.7.1 Themes
- Light and dark modes
- High-contrast modes
- Brand theming and white-labelling
- Density modes

### 12.8 Advanced UI Patterns (Core-Senior)

#### 12.8.1 Complex application interfaces
- Professional tools: editors, canvases, IDE-like layouts, multi-pane workspaces
- Command palettes and keyboard-driven workflows
- Drag-and-drop interactions
- Real-time collaborative interfaces
- Complex permissions and admin interfaces
- Workflow builders and node-based editors (common in agent-building tools)
- Notification systems and inboxes
- Designing for power users and novices simultaneously

## Domain 13: Motion and Micro-interaction Design

**Label:** [Essential] (13.1–13.2), [Core-Senior] (13.3) · **Depth target:** D2 → D3 · **Prerequisites:** Domains 9, 12.

### 13.1 Motion Principles

#### 13.1.1 Why motion
- Functional motion: feedback, orientation, continuity, focus, relationships between elements
- Expressive motion: brand personality, delight
- Motion as hierarchy in time

#### 13.1.2 Motion fundamentals
- Duration (typically 100–500ms for UI; scale with distance and size)
- Easing curves: ease-out for entrances, ease-in for exits, ease-in-out for movement; custom cubic-béziers
- Spring physics: stiffness, damping, mass; natural, interruptible motion
- Choreography and staggering
- Disney's 12 principles of animation applied to UI (squash and stretch, anticipation, follow-through, etc.)
- Spatial models: where things come from and go to
- Reduced motion: alternatives (fades instead of movement); respecting user settings

### 13.2 Micro-interactions

#### 13.2.1 Designing micro-interactions
- Structure: trigger, rules, feedback, loops and modes (Saffer)
- Button presses, toggles, likes, pull-to-refresh, swipe actions
- Loading and progress animation; skeletons; shimmer
- Success and error feedback
- Hover and focus transitions
- Haptic feedback on mobile
- AI-specific motion: streaming text appearance, thinking indicators, agent progress, tool activity — conveying "working" without false precision

### 13.3 Advanced Motion (Core-Senior)

#### 13.3.1 Tools and specification
- Prototyping motion: Figma Smart Animate, Figma's motion tool (announced Config 2026), ProtoPie, Jitter, After Effects + Lottie, Rive (interactive state-machine animation)
- Specifying motion for engineers: duration, easing, delay, properties, spring parameters; motion tokens
- Shared-element transitions and view transitions
- Gesture-driven interactions with interruption and velocity
- Motion systems in design systems

## Domain 19: AI Foundations for Designers

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D2 (conceptual fluency, plus hands-on intuition from direct use) · **Prerequisites:** Domains 4, 9, 10. Domain 16 helps.

**Why it matters:** You cannot design well with a material you do not understand. Designers who know how models work — tokens, context, probability, tools, retrieval — design more realistic, more trustworthy and more cost-aware experiences, and collaborate credibly with AI engineers.

### 19.1 AI Fundamentals

#### 19.1.1 Core vocabulary
- Artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI
- Models, training, inference
- Narrow tasks (classification, recommendation, prediction) vs. general-purpose generative models
- Short history: rules-based systems → statistical ML → deep learning → transformers → chat assistants → reasoning models and agents
- The 2026 landscape: frontier model providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek and others); closed vs. open-weight models; model tiers (fast/cheap vs. most capable)

### 19.2 Machine Learning Basics

#### 19.2.1 How machines learn
- Learning from examples: data, labels, patterns
- Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning (intuitive level)
- Training data determines behaviour; bias in, bias out
- Accuracy is never 100%: false positives and false negatives, and the design consequences of each (which error is worse for users?)
- Precision vs. recall trade-offs as design decisions
- Confidence scores and thresholds
- Feedback loops: how user interactions can become training or evaluation data

### 19.3 Large Language Models

#### 19.3.1 How LLMs work (conceptually)
- Next-token prediction; tokens are word fragments
- Pretraining on large text corpora; instruction tuning and preference training produce assistant behaviour
- Context window: everything the model "sees" at once (instructions, conversation, documents, tool results); models do not remember between sessions unless the product gives them memory
- Knowledge cutoff; retrieval and web search as ways to add fresh information
- Temperature and randomness: why the same prompt gives different outputs
- Reasoning ("thinking") models: slower, more deliberate, better at multi-step problems
- Latency: time to first token vs. total generation time; streaming
- Cost: tokens in and out; why long contexts and long outputs cost more
- System prompts: how products shape model behaviour

#### 19.3.2 Capabilities
- Language understanding and generation in many languages
- Summarizing, transforming, extracting, classifying, rewriting
- Coding and generating interfaces
- Reasoning over documents; answering with citations when grounded
- Tool use: calling functions, searching, acting in software
- Vision: understanding screenshots, images, documents, charts

#### 19.3.3 Limitations
- Hallucination/confabulation: fluent, confident, wrong
- Sycophancy: agreeing with the user
- Inconsistency and prompt sensitivity
- Weak calibration: confidence ≠ correctness
- Limited true memory; context degradation over long sessions
- Bias, stereotyping and uneven performance across languages and cultures
- Vulnerability to manipulation (prompt injection)
- Designing around limitations is the core of AI product design

### 19.4 Generative AI and Multimodal AI

#### 19.4.1 Modalities
- Text generation
- Image generation (diffusion models): prompting, styles, control (reference images, inpainting, outpainting), consistency challenges
- Speech: transcription, text-to-speech, real-time voice conversation
- Video and 3D generation (awareness)
- Vision-language models: understanding UI screenshots, sketches, photos, documents
- Multimodal interaction design implications: input switching, accessibility, privacy (camera, microphone)

#### 19.4.2 Key AI system concepts for designers
- Embeddings and semantic search (meaning-based rather than keyword-based)
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): answering from your documents with citations
- Tool use and function calling: how AI takes actions
- Agents: models working in loops with tools toward goals
- Memory: what is remembered, where, and for how long
- The Model Context Protocol (MCP): a standard way for AI apps to connect to tools and data — and to display interactive UI from those tools (MCP Apps)
- Evaluation ("evals"): how teams measure model quality systematically

### 19.5 Prompt Engineering

#### 19.5.1 Prompting skills
- Clear, specific instructions with context and purpose
- Role and audience framing
- Examples (few-shot) and their influence
- Structuring prompts with sections and delimiters
- Asking for reasoning, alternatives or critique
- Iterating: diagnosing why output missed, then adjusting
- Prompting for visual generation: subject, composition, style, lighting, references, negative constraints
- Prompting for UI generation: describing layout, hierarchy, components, states, design-system constraints, screenshots as reference

### 19.6 Context Engineering

#### 19.6.1 Managing what the model knows
- Context as a finite, curated resource
- Providing the right material: design system docs, brand guidelines, research summaries, screenshots, user data (with consent)
- Reusable context: project instructions, custom assistants, knowledge bases, skills
- Why fresh sessions sometimes outperform long conversations
- For product design: deciding what context the *product* gives its model — user history, preferences, workspace data — is a UX, privacy and quality decision

## Domain 20: AI-Native Product Design

**Label:** [Essential] (20.1–20.11), [Core-Senior] (20.12–20.15) · **Depth target:** D3 (D4 for designers leading AI products) · **Prerequisites:** Domains 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 17, 19.

**Why it matters:** This is the defining capability of the AI-native product designer: designing experiences where probabilistic, autonomous systems collaborate with people — useful, trustworthy, controllable and honest about their limits.

### 20.1 AI-Native Design Principles

#### 20.1.1 Foundational principles
- Start from the user's problem, not the model's capability ("AI-first" ≠ "user-first")
- Augmentation vs. automation: decide which parts of a task people want to keep
- Design for appropriate trust (calibrated reliance), not maximum trust
- Make capabilities and limitations discoverable
- Keep humans in control: easy to steer, correct, undo and dismiss
- Design for failure as a normal state, not an edge case
- Transparency about what the system did, why, and with what information
- Respect privacy, consent and data boundaries
- Fit latency and cost into the experience design
- Reference frameworks:
  - Google PAIR People + AI Guidebook (user needs, mental models, explainability + trust, feedback + control, errors + graceful failure)
  - Microsoft's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (initially, during interaction, when wrong, over time)
  - Apple HIG — Generative AI and Machine Learning guidance
  - IBM Design for AI and Carbon for AI

#### 20.1.2 The new interaction paradigm
- From command-based interaction (telling the computer *how*) to intent-based interaction (telling it *what* outcome you want)
- The articulation barrier: many users struggle to express intent in prose — design scaffolds (suggestions, templates, examples, structured controls)
- Delegation as an interaction model: briefing, monitoring, reviewing, accepting
- Beyond chat: AI embedded in existing workflows, canvases, documents and tools

### 20.2 Deciding When and Where to Use AI

#### 20.2.1 Problem fit
- Tasks well-suited to AI: high variability, language-heavy, tolerant of imperfection, easy to verify, expensive to do manually
- Tasks poorly suited: require perfect accuracy, hard to verify, high stakes without oversight, simple deterministic rules suffice
- The cost of errors: who is harmed, and how recoverable is it?
- Verification cost: if checking takes longer than doing, AI may not help
- Opportunity mapping: identifying AI opportunities in user journeys
- Feasibility conversations with engineers: prototype with real models early

### 20.3 Conversational and Chat Interfaces

#### 20.3.1 Chat design
- When chat is the right interface (open-ended exploration, Q&A, multi-turn refinement) and when it is not (structured, repetitive or visual tasks)
- Anatomy of chat UI: composer, attachments, context selectors, model/mode selectors, message list, message actions, conversation history
- Onboarding: capability discovery, suggested prompts, examples, templates
- Turn-taking, streaming and interruption (stop, edit, retry)
- Multi-turn context: what the assistant remembers in this conversation
- Conversation management: titles, search, projects/folders, sharing, branching
- Rich responses: structured content, tables, code, images, interactive widgets inside conversations
- Artifacts/canvases: side-by-side workspaces for documents, code or designs generated in chat
- Voice conversation: turn detection, interruption, latency, transcripts
- Conversation design craft (Domain 10.4): persona, tone, repair, clarifying questions

### 20.4 Copilots and Embedded Assistance

#### 20.4.1 Copilot patterns
- Inline suggestions and ghost text (accept, reject, partial accept)
- Inline editing on a selection ("rewrite this", "make shorter")
- Side-panel assistants with awareness of the current context
- Smart defaults and auto-fill
- Proactive suggestions: timing, relevance, interruption cost; making them dismissible and tunable
- Summaries and digests
- Natural-language commands in command palettes
- Search with AI answers and citations
- Keeping the user's work primary; AI as a secondary layer

### 20.5 AI Agents, Agentic Workflows and Autonomous Experiences

#### 20.5.1 Designing for agents
- What agents do: plan, use tools, act over multiple steps, sometimes for minutes or hours
- Spectrum of autonomy: suggest → draft → act with confirmation → act and report → fully autonomous within limits
- Briefing agents: goal setting, constraints, context, success criteria — designing the "task brief" experience
- Plans: showing a proposed plan; letting users edit before execution
- Progress and status: step lists, live activity, what the agent is doing now, elapsed time, estimated remaining work — avoiding false precision
- Background agents: notifications, task inboxes, resuming context when the user returns
- Reviewing outcomes: diffs, before/after, summaries of changes, links to evidence
- Interrupting, pausing, redirecting and cancelling
- Handoffs between agent and human (and between agents)
- Multi-agent experiences: representing several agents working in parallel without overwhelming users
- Agent identity and accountability: which agent did what, on whose behalf, with which permissions
- Agents acting in shared spaces (e.g., agents as teammates in issue trackers, docs, chat) — conventions such as Linear's Agent Interaction Guidelines

#### 20.5.2 Autonomous experiences
- Setting boundaries: budgets, permissions, scopes, time limits
- Delegated authority and consent: what the agent may do without asking
- Audit trails and activity logs
- Reversibility as a precondition for autonomy

### 20.6 Human-in-the-Loop Interactions

#### 20.6.1 Control and oversight
- Approval moments for consequential actions (purchases, sending messages, deleting data, publishing)
- Designing approvals that are informative, not rubber stamps (clear consequences, diffs, risk signals)
- Approval fatigue and how to calibrate frequency (risk-based approvals, "always allow for this session")
- Editing AI output before it is used
- Escalation to human experts or support
- Override and correction mechanisms that improve future behaviour
- Accountability: making clear who is responsible for the final decision

### 20.7 AI-Generated Content and Generative UI

#### 20.7.1 Generated content
- Presenting generated text, images, code and data
- Variations and selection (multiple options vs. one best answer)
- Iteration loops: refine, remix, regenerate, adjust parameters (tuners/sliders for tone, length, style)
- Attribution and disclosure: labelling AI-generated content; provenance standards (C2PA content credentials — awareness)
- Copyright, ownership and training-data concerns
- Quality control for generated media; brand consistency

#### 20.7.2 Generative and adaptive UI
- Interfaces composed at runtime by models (choosing components, building views)
- Constraining generation with design systems so results stay consistent and accessible
- Stability vs. adaptivity: avoiding interfaces that change unpredictably
- Interactive components inside AI conversations and in MCP Apps / ChatGPT apps
- Malleable software: users shaping their own tools with AI

### 20.8 AI-Powered Personalization and Memory

#### 20.8.1 Personalization
- Personalization based on behaviour, preferences and explicit memory
- Transparency: "why am I seeing this?"
- Control: viewing, editing and deleting memories; incognito/temporary modes
- Filter bubbles, creepiness thresholds and consent
- Cold start experiences
- Personalization across devices and contexts

### 20.9 Explainability, Trust and Transparency

#### 20.9.1 Explainability
- Levels of explanation: what it did, why, based on what, how confident
- Citations and source linking; showing the evidence
- Showing reasoning (summarized thinking, step lists) — benefits and risks (users may over-trust plausible reasoning)
- Global explanations (how the system works in general) vs. local explanations (why this output)
- Model and data cards; AI disclosures; "how this feature works" pages

#### 20.9.2 Trust and transparency
- Trust calibration: avoiding both over-reliance (automation bias) and under-reliance
- Signals of reliability: consistency, provenance, track record, honest limitations
- Anthropomorphism: when human-like personas help and when they mislead
- Disclosure that users are interacting with AI (and regulatory requirements such as the EU AI Act's transparency obligations)
- Privacy transparency: what data is used, stored or shared with model providers

### 20.10 Uncertainty and Failure States

#### 20.10.1 Designing for imperfection
- Communicating uncertainty: confidence language, ranges, multiple options, "verify this" prompts — without numerical false precision
- Failure taxonomy: no answer, wrong answer, partial answer, refusal, timeout, tool failure, rate limit, policy block, hallucinated citation, off-task action
- Graceful degradation: fallbacks to non-AI paths; manual alternatives always available
- Error messages for AI: honest, specific, actionable
- Refusals that explain and redirect
- Recovering from agent mistakes: undo, rollback, compensation actions
- Latency states: thinking, searching, working — progressive disclosure of progress
- Designing for variability: the same input can produce different outputs; set expectations

### 20.11 AI Feedback Mechanisms

#### 20.11.1 Feedback loops
- Explicit feedback: thumbs up/down, ratings, flags, written comments, "report a problem"
- Implicit feedback: accept/reject, edits, copy, regenerate, abandonment
- Designing feedback that users actually give (lightweight, contextual, rewarding)
- Closing the loop: showing users their feedback mattered
- Using feedback for evaluation and improvement (with consent and privacy safeguards)
- Correcting the AI in context ("that's wrong — use X") and remembering corrections

### 20.12 Designing for Non-Deterministic Systems: Quality and Evaluation (Core-Senior)

#### 20.12.1 Designers and evals
- Why static mockups cannot represent AI behaviour: designing with real outputs
- Defining quality: rubrics for helpfulness, accuracy, tone, format, safety, brand voice
- Building example sets: representative, edge-case and adversarial inputs
- Error analysis: reviewing real conversations/traces with engineers; categorizing failures
- Shaping model behaviour: contributing to system prompts, response guidelines, examples and tool descriptions
- Evaluating designs across many runs, not one golden output
- Measuring AI UX: acceptance rate, edit rate, task success, time saved, trust calibration, satisfaction

### 20.13 AI Interaction Patterns (Core-Senior)

#### 20.13.1 Pattern vocabulary (after *Shape of AI* and related libraries)
- **Wayfinders:** suggestions, templates, examples, follow-up prompts, nudges, gallery of starting points
- **Prompt actions:** open input, inline actions, auto-fill, transform, expand, summarize, restyle, remix/blend, synthesis
- **Tuners:** attachments, parameters, modes, model selection, preset styles, filters, voice and tone controls, references
- **Governors:** action plans, controls, branches, citations, draft mode, variations, verification, memory, sample response, stream of thought
- **Trust builders:** disclosure, caveats, consent, data ownership, footprints/provenance, incognito mode
- **Identifiers:** AI naming, avatars, color and iconography for AI (and when to avoid special "AI sparkle" treatment)
- **Dark patterns of AI:** fake personalization, manipulative companionship, unclear data use, artificial urgency, hiding the off-switch

### 20.14 Multimodal, Voice and Spatial AI Experiences (Core-Senior)

#### 20.14.1 Beyond text
- Voice assistants and real-time voice agents: turn-taking, barge-in, confirmations, earcons, transcripts
- Camera-based AI (visual search, live assistance)
- AI in wearables and ambient devices (awareness)
- Spatial computing with AI (visionOS, XR — awareness)
- Accessibility across modalities

### 20.15 Responsible AI and Safety by Design (Core-Senior)

#### 20.15.1 Responsible design
- Harm identification: misinformation, bias, exclusion, over-reliance, manipulation, privacy violations, emotional dependency
- Designing for vulnerable users (minors, people in crisis)
- Safety interventions: guardrails, friction for risky actions, safe completions, crisis resources
- Fairness testing across demographic groups and languages
- Consent and data governance
- Regulation awareness: EU AI Act risk categories and transparency obligations; sector rules (health, finance, employment)
- Red-teaming workshops with cross-functional teams
- AI ethics frameworks from industry and research

## Domain 21: AI-Assisted Design Workflows

**Label:** [Essential] · **Depth target:** D3 · **Prerequisites:** Domains 7–16, 19. Introduced lightly earlier; mastered here.

**Why it matters:** AI compresses the distance between idea and testable experience. Designers who direct AI well explore more widely, prototype in real code, and spend more time on judgment and user contact. Designers who use it uncritically ship generic, inaccessible, unvalidated work faster.

### 21.1 AI-Assisted Research

#### 21.1.1 Research with AI
- Desk research: summarizing markets, competitors, academic research — with source verification
- Planning: drafting research plans, screeners, discussion guides (then refining them yourself)
- Interview operations: transcription, translation, note-taking, tagging
- Synthesis assistance: clustering, theme drafting, quote extraction — validated against raw data
- Survey analysis of open-ended responses
- Ethical boundaries: participant consent for AI processing, data privacy, never fabricating quotes or "synthetic users" as evidence
- Tools: Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini with projects and file uploads; Dovetail, Maze, Notion AI and similar research platforms with AI features (awareness)

### 21.2 AI-Powered Ideation and Visual Exploration

#### 21.2.1 Ideation
- Generating many concepts, reframings, analogies and "how might we" variations
- Role-playing stakeholder or user perspectives (as provocation, not evidence)
- Scenario and edge-case generation
- Critiquing your concepts against heuristics and principles

#### 21.2.2 Visual exploration
- Moodboards and art direction with image models (Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, Krea, image features in ChatGPT/Gemini)
- Style references, consistency controls, iteration
- Generating illustrations, icons and imagery — rights, licensing and disclosure
- Exploring layout and visual directions quickly; curating with taste

### 21.3 AI Prototyping

#### 21.3.1 Prompt-to-prototype tools
- Figma Make (prompt-to-app inside Figma, using your design context)
- v0 (Vercel), Lovable, Bolt, Replit — prompt-to-app generators
- Google Stitch (prompt-to-UI)
- Claude artifacts and ChatGPT canvas for quick interactive prototypes
- Framer AI for websites
- Strengths: speed, real interactivity, real data, real AI behaviour
- Weaknesses: generic aesthetics, inaccessible markup, inconsistent states, code quality, design-system drift

#### 21.3.2 Prototyping in code with AI coding agents
- Using Cursor, Claude Code or Codex with a real codebase or starter (React + Tailwind + shadcn/ui)
- Briefing: describing intent, layout, states, interactions, constraints; attaching screenshots and Figma links (via the Figma MCP server)
- Iterating visually: screenshots, browser previews, comparing to designs
- Prototyping AI features with real model calls (streaming, tools, failure cases)
- Managing prototypes: Git basics, preview deployments, sharing links for testing
- Knowing when a prototype should become production code (and handing over responsibly)

### 21.4 Design Generation and Systems

#### 21.4.1 Generating designs
- Generating first drafts of screens and components inside Figma (Figma agent and AI features) and in external tools
- Keeping generation on-system: design system context, tokens, components, Code Connect, MCP
- Generating content variations, realistic data and localization drafts
- Batch tasks: renaming layers, organizing files, creating variants, generating documentation

### 21.5 AI Design Critique

#### 21.5.1 Critique with AI
- Using models for heuristic reviews, accessibility pre-checks, copy reviews and consistency checks
- Asking for specific critiques (hierarchy, contrast, clarity of primary action) rather than "is this good?"
- Recognizing generic or wrong feedback; models praise too easily (sycophancy)
- Combining AI critique with human critique and user testing

### 21.6 AI-Assisted Documentation and Communication

#### 21.6.1 Documentation
- Drafting design rationale, specs, PRDs, component documentation, release notes
- Summarizing meetings and decisions
- Generating annotations and handoff notes — verifying accuracy
- Writing documentation that AI agents can use (structured, explicit, current)

### 21.7 Design-to-Code Workflows

#### 21.7.1 Bridging design and code with AI
- Figma Dev Mode + MCP server → coding agents generating components faithful to designs
- Code Connect mapping design components to real components
- Figma code layers (Config 2026) and code-backed prototypes on the canvas (current tools)
- Designer-authored pull requests for UI polish (with engineering review)
- Design QA with AI: screenshot comparisons, automated visual and accessibility checks

### 21.8 AI Agent Workflows for Designers

#### 21.8.1 Delegating design operations
- Agents that run research ops (recruiting logistics, scheduling, synthesis drafts)
- Agents that audit design systems (unused components, inconsistent tokens)
- Agents that produce variations for experiments
- Custom assistants, skills and project instructions encoding your team's design standards
- Supervising agents: reviewing outputs, setting boundaries, maintaining accountability

### 21.10 Judgment: What Not to Delegate

#### 21.10.1 Keeping the human core
- Problem framing and deciding what matters
- Direct contact with users
- Taste and final visual/interaction decisions
- Ethical judgment and accountability
- Building your own craft: deliberate practice without AI to keep skills sharp
